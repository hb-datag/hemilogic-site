Active Tremor Online Mitigation Sleeve

A forearm sleeve in development that reads the motor signal at the elbow, predicts the tremor riding on it, and delivers an out of phase impulse so the wearer's intended movement comes through.

The problem

Hand tremor takes away ordinary tasks

Tremor makes the hand move when the person holding it wants stillness. Writing a signature, holding a cup, using a key, fastening a button, eating in company. These are small acts that carry a lot of independence with them, and tremor takes them one at a time.

Medication helps many people and its effect changes across the day. Surgical options exist for some patients and are invasive. Weighted and damped utensils address a single task at a time. There is room for a wearable that works on the signal itself and travels with the person through a normal day.

What we are building toward. A sleeve the wearer puts on in the morning, that keeps working while they move through the day, and that measures its own effect well enough for a clinician to see what it did. ATOMS is in development and has not been tested in patients.

How it works

A closed loop around the forearm

We think of tremor the way an audio engineer thinks of unwanted sound. The tremor is one component of a signal that also carries what the person means to do. Our aim is to cancel the first and pass the second.

01

Read at the elbow

The elbow sensor picks up the motor signal on its way to the hand, before the movement has happened.

02

Separate and predict

Our model splits the intended movement from the tremor component and predicts the phase the tremor will be in next.

03

Counteract

Conductive wire woven into the elastic sleeve delivers an out of phase impulse across the forearm, so the tremor component is opposed and the intended signal passes through.

04

Measure and tune

A wrist accelerometer measures what the hand actually did and feeds that back, so the model keeps tuning itself to this wearer.

The ATOMS loop. Two sensor sites, one actuator, running continuously.

The sleeve

Elastic fabric with conductive wire woven through it

The sleeve is worn on the forearm. Conductive wire is woven into the elastic weave itself, so the fabric carries the impulse and there is no rigid housing strapped to the arm. The elastic holds the wire against the skin as the arm moves.

ATOMS uses two of the seven HemiSense sites. The elbow sensor sits above the sleeve and reads the incoming signal. The wrist sensor sits below it and reports what the hand did. Everything between them is fabric.

  • Worn on the forearm, under a sleeve or over it
  • Conductive wire woven into elastic fabric
  • Two sensor sites only, elbow and wrist
  • No rigid enclosure on the moving segment
  • Designed to be put on and taken off without help

The platform

Built on the HemiSense Array

The HemiSense Array is our wearable sensor network. Seven sites read nervous system signals and interpret them so they can drive assistive devices. A product uses the sites its job needs. ATOMS uses two.

  • 1 Headband
  • 2 Elbow ATOMS
  • 3 Wrist ATOMS
  • 4 Chest strap
  • 5 Waist belt
  • 6 Knee
  • 7 Ankle

Detect sits with the sensors. The HemiSense sensors themselves are being developed to detect early signs of tremor, including the kind seen in Parkinson's disease. That detection capability belongs to the array. ATOMS carries Monitor, Treat and Predict.

Exploratory

One array, many products

These are directions we are exploring on the HemiSense Array. None of them is in development today, and we are naming them here so it is clear where the platform can go after ATOMS.

  • Stroke detection: prompt the wearer to open and close their hands, analyze the signal, and take appropriate action
  • Smart prosthetic and robotic hand control
  • Powered wheelchair control
  • Computer control
  • Drop foot device control
