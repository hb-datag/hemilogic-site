A sleeve that quiets hand tremor, and the brain that runs it.

HemiLogic builds wearable technology for people whose nervous systems have stopped carrying signals faithfully. Our first product is SteadyHand, a soft forearm sleeve for people whose hands shake. It reads the signal on its way to the hand, tells the intended movement apart from the tremor, and answers the tremor with a small signal of its own. It is in development now, and it has not yet been tested in patients.

What it does

Three things happen inside the sleeve, over and over, faster than you can notice.

Tremor is a signal problem before it is a movement problem. The brain sends the hand an instruction, and an unwanted rhythm rides along with it. The hand obeys both. SteadyHand works at the point where that signal passes through the forearm, which is why it can act before the hand has moved.

It listens

A sensor at the elbow reads the signal your nervous system sends to your hand, before the hand moves. It reads continuously, all day, without you doing anything.

It tells the difference

Software inside the sleeve separates the movement you meant to make from the tremor riding along with it, and predicts what the tremor will do in the next fraction of a second.

It answers back

Fine conductive thread woven into the fabric sends a small signal of its own, timed to push against the tremor, so the movement you meant comes through and the shake is opposed.

No medication. No surgery. No box on a belt. The sleeve works on the signal itself, using thread and two sensors. A sensor at the wrist measures what your hand actually did and feeds that back, so the sleeve keeps tuning itself to you, this day, this hour.
Your doctor sees what happened. Everything the sleeve measures is recorded, and every decision the software makes can be read as a plain rule: this signal, this pattern, this response. We call that explainable AI: intelligence that shows its reasoning in words a clinician can check.

How it fits together

A brain, and a body.

Every product we make has the same two parts. One part senses and understands. The other part acts. Keeping them separate is what lets us build one brain carefully and then give it many different hands.

THE BRAIN

HemiSense Array

A set of sensors worn on the body that read the nervous system and understand what they read. On its own, with nothing attached, the array can spot a condition, follow it over time, and see where it is heading. Every conclusion it reaches can be read as a plain rule.

THE BODY

HemiAct add-ons

Devices that plug into the array and act on what it knows. SteadyHand is the first: it borrows the array's elbow and wrist sensors and adds the woven thread that pushes back. Others will follow, each built for a different job, all sharing the same brain.

We are building the elbow and wrist sensors and the SteadyHand sleeve first, because tremor is measurable, common, and poorly served. The rest of the array, and the add-ons after it, come one at a time, as the evidence allows.

Who it is for

We are starting with four conditions.

Each one is a way the nervous system stops carrying signals faithfully. Each one leaves a signature a wearable sensor can read. The sleeve comes first. The same brain is built to reach the rest.

Hands that shake

Parkinson's disease and essential tremor

Tremor, stiffness, slow movement, and the way symptoms rise and fall as medication wears on and off across a day.

Seizures

Epilepsy

Knowing when a seizure is happening, and the harder question underneath it, knowing one is coming before it starts.

Memory loss

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias

Catching the earliest changes, and following how they progress, in the place a person actually lives rather than a clinic visit twice a year.

Recovering from a stroke

Neurorehabilitation

Measuring recovery objectively between therapy sessions, so progress is seen and not guessed.

Our mission

To build a wearable brain that reads the nervous system, a body of add-ons that act on what it reads, and to show the reasoning plainly enough that a doctor can check it.

We hold ourselves to a simple standard. If a device cannot explain why it did what it did, in words a clinician would accept in a chart note, it is not finished.
