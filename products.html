Products

The array is the brain. The add-ons are the body.

Everything we make has two parts. The HemiSense Array is a wearable sensor network that reads the nervous system, understands what it reads, and explains itself. On its own it can detect, monitor and predict. HemiAct add-ons plug into the array and act on what it knows. SteadyHand, a sleeve that quiets hand tremor, is the first of them.

The brain

The HemiSense Array

A wearable network of eleven sensors at seven sites on the body, in development. It reads signals from the brain, the nerves, the muscles and the heart, and it turns them into something a person can act on and a doctor can read. It is a product in its own right. Worn alone, with no add-on attached, it does three things.

Detect

Find the signature of a neurological condition in daily life, including early signs that a twice-yearly visit would miss. Ask the wearer to open and close their hands, read the signal, and know what it means.

Monitor

Follow a condition over weeks and months on a continuous scale, so a clinician sees the whole record rather than a description from memory.

Predict

Model where things are heading and how soon, with the confidence and the reasons stated in plain rules rather than implied by a score.

Seven sites. Three on the midline, four worn on both sides. Eleven sensors in all.

  • 1 Headband midline
  • 2 Elbow, both arms building first
  • 3 Wrist, both arms building first
  • 4 Chest strap midline
  • 5 Waist belt midline
  • 6 Knee, both legs
  • 7 Ankle, both legs

Modular sensors for personalized care. A wearer puts on only the sites their care needs. A person with tremor needs the arm. A person with a gait problem would wear the waist, knees and ankles. A person with seizures would wear the headband. The software and the way it explains itself stay the same across all of them.

What we are building first. The elbow and wrist sensors, and the SteadyHand sleeve that runs on them. The other five sites come after, one at a time, as the evidence allows.

The body

HemiAct add-ons

The array knows what is happening. A HemiAct add-on is anything that does something about it. Each one plugs into the array, borrows its senses and its judgment, and carries out an action: a signal to a muscle, a dose of a medicine, a command to a machine. The add-on carries the fourth function, Treat, and every add-on inherits the same rule: what it does can be read and checked by a doctor.

First, in development

SteadyHand sleeves

A forearm sleeve that opposes tremor with a small signal timed against it. Uses the elbow and wrist sites. The full description is below.

Future, exploratory

Adaptive dosing

An add-on that gives a medicine in the amount the body needs at that moment, read from the array, so a person is neither over nor under medicated across a day. This is an idea we are exploring. It is not in development.

Future, exploratory

Interfaces to other devices

The array can speak to machines other people build. We see a path to controlling a computer, a powered wheelchair, a drop foot device, or a robotic prosthetic hand from the signals the array already reads.

First add-on

SteadyHand sleeves

A forearm sleeve, in development, for people whose hands shake. It reads the signal your nervous system sends to your hand, predicts the tremor riding on that signal, and answers with a small signal of its own, timed to push against the shake so the movement you meant comes through.

It is soft, it is worn like a compression sleeve, and it has no wires running to anything else. The two round sensors are the HemiSense Array at the elbow and the wrist. The sleeve between them is the HemiAct add-on: elastic fabric with conductive thread woven through it, which is what delivers the counter-signal.

Design rendering. Sensor at the elbow, sensor at the wrist, woven thread between. Not yet a manufactured prototype.

The loop, four steps

01

Read at the elbow

The array's elbow sensor picks up the motor signal on its way to the hand, before the hand has moved.

02

Separate and predict

The array splits the movement you intended from the tremor riding on it, and predicts the phase the tremor will be in next.

03

Push back

Fine conductive wire woven into the elastic fabric delivers a small impulse across the forearm, timed out of phase with the tremor, so the tremor is opposed and the intended signal passes through. This is the add-on's job.

04

Check and tune

The array's wrist sensor measures what the hand actually did and feeds that back, so the model keeps tuning itself to this wearer, this day, this hour.

The SteadyHand loop. Two array sites, one add-on, running continuously. The sensors and the software belong to the array. The impulse belongs to the sleeve.

What it is made of

An elastic sleeve worn on the forearm. Conductive thread is woven into the fabric itself, so the sleeve carries the impulse and there is no rigid housing strapped to a moving arm. The elastic holds the thread against the skin as the arm bends and turns, and the open weave lets the skin breathe under it for a full day. Two array sensors, one at the elbow and one at the wrist, each a flat disc that sits in the fabric. Everything between them is fabric.

It is designed to be put on in the morning, without help, with one hand if the other is shaking, and then left alone.

Where it stands. SteadyHand is in development. It has not been tested in patients. It is not for sale, and it is not cleared or approved by any regulator. We are working toward a first feasibility study with clinical partners in movement disorders. When that begins, this page will say so.

How it grows

More senses, then more hands.

The array grows one site at a time. Each new site adds something the brain of the system can read: the headband for seizures and cognition, the waist and legs for gait. The add-ons grow alongside it. Each new HemiAct add-on takes what the array already knows and puts it to a new use.

We do not start over for each condition. We add a sense, we write the rules for it, and we build the hand that acts on it. Everything underneath stays the same, including the promise that it can explain itself.

In plain terms. Brain first, then body. The HemiSense Array is the product we are building the company on. SteadyHand is the first thing it does with its hands.
